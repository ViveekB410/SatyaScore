1. Executive Summary
Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4, Gemini, and Llama are increasingly deployed in critical domains
including healthcare, law, education, and governance. A persistent and dangerous failure mode of these models
is hallucination — the generation of plausible but factually incorrect or fabricated content, delivered with high
confidence.
This project proposes the design, development, and evaluation of SatyaScore, a claim-level Hallucination
Detection and Scoring Framework with English and Hindi as core, committed languages, and Marathi as a
tiered stretch goal (a smaller ~800–1,200 claim dataset, explicitly ranked below the core deliverables — see
Section 15). The system decomposes LLM-generated text into atomic factual claims, verifies each claim against
a structured knowledge base, and produces a graded (continuous) hallucination confidence score at the
individual-claim level — rather than a binary label or an approximate error span.
The core technical contribution is the Composite Multilingual Hallucination Scoring Algorithm (CMHSA),
which fuses three independent verification signals — cross-lingual semantic similarity, natural language
inference (NLI) entailment, and knowledge-graph consistency verification (Wikidata) — into a single interpretable
score. This is, to the best of current literature, the first system to combine all three signal types at the claim level
for Hindi or English, a combination absent from Mu-SHROOM (similarity/entailment/voting only, no KG), SHROOM￾CAP (binary labels, no KG evidence, no graded score), and more recent knowledge-graph-grounded work —
MultiHal, FactKG, and KGR — none of which fuse all three signals into one deployable, claim-level score for
Hindi (see Section 3 and Section 17 Category E for full differentiation). This dual-purpose project targets both a
Provisional Indian Patent application and a research paper submission to a peer-reviewed NLP venue (IEEE
Access, or an ACL-affiliated workshop track appropriate for a student capstone).

2. Problem Statement & Motivation
2.1 The Hallucination Problem
Hallucination in LLMs refers to the generation of confident, syntactically fluent, yet factually incorrect or entirely
fabricated content. Reported hallucination rates vary widely (roughly 15%–40% of factual queries) depending on
domain, model, and language — and rates are generally worse for lower-resource languages such as Hindi and
Marathi, where training data is comparatively sparse.
Current, still-unresolved challenges in hallucination detection include: - No production-ready, real-time
hallucination detector exists that is both claim-level and knowledge-graph-grounded, for English or Hindi. -
Existing multilingual systems either mark approximate error locations (span-level, Mu-SHROOM) or apply a
binary correct/incorrect label (SHROOM-CAP) — neither produces a graded, decision-support-ready confidence
score. - No existing system combines semantic similarity, NLI entailment, and structured knowledge-graph
verification into one fused score at the claim level, for any Indian language including Hindi. - Marathi — spoken
by 80+ million people — has zero coverage in any major multilingual hallucination benchmark to date; this
project addresses it at a smaller, tiered scale (Section 15) rather than full parity with Hindi/English, given the
timeline.
2.2 Why This Matters
India has 500M+ smartphone users consuming information in regional languages via LLM-powered apps.
Government services, healthcare advisory, and legal information delivered via AI chatbots can cause
serious harm if hallucinated content goes undetected.
No Indian patent currently exists for a claim-level, knowledge-graph-grounded LLM hallucination scoring
system for Hindi — a genuine IP opportunity remains open around the KG-fusion approach itself,
independent of language coverage.

4.2 What the System Does
Given any LLM-generated text in English or Hindi (Marathi planned as future extension), the system: 1. Extracts
atomic factual claims from the text (Claim Extractor module). 2. Embeds each claim using cross-lingual
multilingual embeddings (XLM-R primary, MuRIL fallback for Hindi). 3. Retrieves top-k relevant knowledge
passages from a multilingual FAISS vector store, plus Wikidata SPARQL triples. 4. Computes a composite
hallucination score per claim using NLI + semantic similarity + KG grounding (CMHSA). 5. Returns a structured
JSON report with per-claim verdicts: Verified / Uncertain / Hallucinated. 6. Visualises results on an interactive
Streamlit dashboard with claim-level highlighting.

7.2 The CMHSA Scoring Formula (Patent Core)
HalluScore(c) = α · SemSim(c, E) + β · NLI_prob(c, E) + γ · KG_conf(c, G)
Where: - SemSim(c, E) — Cosine similarity between claim embedding and top-k evidence embeddings (0–1). -
NLI_prob(c, E) — Entailment probability from a fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3 NLI model, extended to Hindi via
IndicXNLI fine-tuning (0–1). - KG_conf(c, G) — Knowledge-graph confidence from Wikidata SPARQL triple
verification (0–1). Wikidata’s Hindi-language entity and label coverage is narrower than English; the team should
budget time to test fallback behaviour (e.g., matching against English-labelled entities when a Hindi label is
missing) and document this limitation explicitly. (This same gap is expected to be more severe for Marathi,
reinforcing the decision to treat it as future work.) - α, β, γ — Language-specific trained weights, learned via a
===== PAGE 7 =====
softmax-constrained linear model (parameterized so α + β + γ = 1 by construction) fit on annotated training
data by minimizing cross-entropy against gold verdict labels — not plain unconstrained logistic regression, which
does not naturally enforce this constraint. - Threshold: Score < 0.35 = Hallucinated | 0.35–0.65 = Uncertain | >
0.65 = Verified.

8. Core Functional Requirements
FR-01: Claim Extraction Module - SHALL accept plain text input in English or Hindi (up to 2,000 words). -
SHALL use dependency parsing (spaCy + IndicNLP) to extract atomic factual claims. - SHALL resolve
coreferences before extraction (e.g., replace pronouns with entity names). - SHALL output a structured list of
claims with character-level span offsets in the original text. - SHALL handle a minimum of 50 claims per
document without performance degradation.
FR-02: Cross-Lingual Embedder - SHALL embed claims using XLM-R-large as the primary model (768-dim
vectors). - SHALL use MuRIL as a fallback model for Hindi-specific inputs, particularly transliterated (Latin-script)
text. - SHALL support batch embedding of up to 128 claims per forward pass. - SHALL normalise embeddings to
unit vectors before similarity computation.
FR-03: Multilingual Knowledge Retriever - SHALL maintain a FAISS IVF index of multilingual Wikipedia
passages, sized appropriately per language rather than a single blanket target — English Wikipedia alone can
supply several million passages, but Hindi and Marathi Wikipedia are considerably smaller, so the realistic
combined target is expected to be lower (to be confirmed during Phase 1 corpus profiling) rather than assumed
at 5 million. - SHALL retrieve top-10 candidate passages per claim within 100ms. - SHALL query the Wikidata
SPARQL API for entity-linked triple verification, with an explicit fallback to English-labelled entities when a Hindi
label is unavailable. - SHALL support index updates without a full rebuild (incremental indexing). - SHALL cache
SPARQL query results with a 24-hour TTL to reduce API calls.
FR-04: CMHSA Scoring Engine (Core) - SHALL compute three sub-scores per claim: SemSim, NLI_prob,
KG_conf. - SHALL apply language-specific weights (α, β, γ) learned during training. - SHALL output a final
HalluScore in [0.0, 1.0] with a confidence interval. - SHALL classify claims into three verdict tiers: Verified
(>0.65), Uncertain (0.35–0.65), Hallucinated (<0.35). - SHALL support custom threshold configuration via API
parameters. - SHALL support an ablation mode (toggle individual signals on/off) to reproduce the paper’s
ablation study directly from the API.
FR-05: REST API (FastAPI) - SHALL expose a POST /detect endpoint accepting JSON with fields: text (str),
language (str), threshold (float, optional). - SHALL return JSON with: claims (list), scores (list), verdicts (list),
overall_score (float), processing_time_ms (int). - SHALL include API key authentication and rate limiting (100
req/min per key). - SHALL provide Swagger/OpenAPI documentation at /docs. - SHALL return HTTP 422 with a
===== PAGE 8 =====
descriptive error for invalid inputs.
FR-06: Web Demo Dashboard - SHALL provide a Streamlit-based web interface accessible at localhost:8501. -
SHALL highlight hallucinated claims in RED, uncertain in AMBER, verified in GREEN within the original text. -
SHALL display a bar chart of HalluScore distributions across all claims. - SHALL allow users to export results as
CSV or JSON. - SHALL support file upload (.txt, .pdf) in addition to text pasting.
FR-07: Benchmark Dataset - SHALL contain ≥ 3,000 claim-level examples across English and Hindi. - SHALL
be balanced: ~40% Hallucinated, ~30% Uncertain, ~30% Verified. - SHALL include inter-annotator agreement
scores (target κ ≥ 0.65, stretch ≥ 0.75). - SHALL be released publicly on HuggingFace Datasets under CC-BY-4.0
license, with an explicit data card describing the combined semantic+NLI+KG annotation scheme.

Creating this dataset is the most important research contribution. Without it, the claim-level, KG-grounded
novelty claim is unsubstantiated.

History, Science, Government, and Sports domains. (Tiered stretch, Phase 4): an additional 400-600
Marathi questions, substantially accelerated by drawing from MahaSQuAD/IndicSQuAD rather than writing
from scratch.
Step 2 — LLM Response Generation: Feed queries to GPT-4, Llama 3, and Gemini. Each generates a
multi-sentence response = potential hallucinations.
Step 3 — Claim Decomposition: Use the Claim Extractor module to decompose responses into atomic
claims (~3,000+ claims total).
Step 4 — Expert Annotation: 2–3 annotators label each claim as Verified / Uncertain / Hallucinated, with
an evidence URL.
Step 5 — Pilot Round (New in v2.0): Before full-scale annotation, run a 50–100 claim pilot batch
specifically to test annotator agreement and refine guidelines — informed directly by Mu-SHROOM’s
documented annotator disagreement on span boundaries.
Step 6 — IAA Calculation: Compute Cohen’s Kappa between annotator pairs. Resolve disagreements by
majority vote or adjudication.
Step 7 — Dataset Release: Upload to HuggingFace Datasets under CC-BY-4.0, with a data card and
paper citation, explicitly noting the combined semantic+NLI+KG claim-level annotation scheme as the
dataset’s key distinguishing feature.
11.3 Annotation Guidelines Summary
Verified: Claim is factually accurate and confirmed by ≥ 2 independent sources.
Hallucinated: Claim contains specific false or fabricated information contradicted by sources.
Uncertain: Claim cannot be confirmed or denied due to ambiguity, opinion, or lack of sources.

15.1 Marathi: Tiered Stretch-Goal Plan (Revised in v2.3)
Marathi is now a committed but explicitly lower-priority tier, not a core deliverable and not pure future￾work — the distinction matters: unlike v2.1/v2.2, the team will attempt real annotation and evaluation this cycle,
sequenced so it never displaces the English/Hindi core goals or the ablation study.
Reusable components: XLM-R already covers Marathi out of the box; MuRIL was explicitly pre-trained on
Marathi among its 17 languages, so the embedding layer needs no architectural change.
Reusable text/QA resources: L3Cube-MahaCorpus (≈24.8M Marathi sentences) can seed the Marathi
portion of the FAISS index; MahaSQuAD and IndicSQuAD provide existing Marathi factual QA pairs that can
accelerate query generation instead of writing 500+ Marathi questions from scratch; IndicXNLI already
includes Marathi entailment data for NLI_prob fine-tuning.
Scope for this cycle: 800–1,200 annotated claims (vs. 3,000 for English/Hindi), using annotation
guidelines already refined from the Hindi pilot round (Phase 2) rather than developing separate guidelines
from zero. One dedicated Marathi-fluent annotator, recruited in Phase 1.
What happens if time runs short: Per Section 12’s drop order, the Marathi tier is the first thing cut back
— reverting cleanly to pure future-work status (as described in earlier PRD versions) with zero impact on
core deliverables. This is a deliberate design choice, not a risk being hidden.
Recommended framing for the synopsis and paper: Present Marathi results as a clearly-labeled
secondary/stretch finding — e.g., “a smaller-scale Marathi evaluation, included as a lower-priority
extension” — rather than claiming full parity with the English/Hindi benchmark. This is both intellectually
honest and strengthens the “extensibility” argument in the patent filing (Section 14), since it demonstrates
the architecture generalizing in practice, not just in theory.
16. Ethical & Responsible-AI Considerations
Domain sensitivity: Since target use cases include healthcare and government-advisory content, the
team should avoid using real, potentially sensitive medical or legal queries during dataset construction
without appropriate care; synthetic/generic factual queries are preferred over queries mimicking real
patient or citizen scenarios.
Dataset bias: Annotators’ own linguistic and regional backgrounds may influence what counts as
“common knowledge” in Hindi-language contexts — guidelines should be reviewed for cultural neutrality.
Transparency in limitations: Any published paper or patent filing should transparently state the KG
coverage gap between English and Hindi (and the likely larger gap for Marathi), and should clearly label
Marathi results as a smaller-scale, lower-priority tier rather than implying full parity with the English/Hindi
benchmark.
Dual-use consideration: A hallucination detector could in principle be used to identify weaknesses in a
competitor’s LLM for adversarial purposes; the team should frame the tool’s intended use as
defensive/auditing, consistent with its healthcare/governance motivation.

general-domain system. No reviewed paper — including this new category — combines all four of these together.
